This was a very design- and programming-heavy week, as I completely redesigned the underlying system that manages the rope dart (again!), before implementing some long-awaited core rope dart features and responding to feedback from last week’s demo. 


### Critical Flaws: Scale

Coming into this week, my main goal was to finish implementing the rope dart features that didn’t make it into the first demo, namely the “wrap” mechanic and how casting the dart can unravel wraps. I had already started building a graph system to keep track of which inputs can be used based on the rope’s current state, but I quickly found critical flaws that ultimately forced me to redesign the whole thing. 

In the old system, each node in the graph represented an abstract maneuver the player could perform, like “Spin”, “Elbow Twine”, or “Dragon Wrap”. However, based on factors like the dart’s rotation direction, the player’s facing direction, different moves may or may not be possible from that node. For example, the player can use a dragon wrap from a downward spin, but not an upward spin. In order to represent these varied interactions for all combinations of factors, I would have needed an exponentially increasing number of nodes. 


### Critical Flaws: Function

In addition to not being sustainable, I realized that the old system couldn’t properly replicate behaviors that occur when a rope dart practitioner casts the dart while in a wrap. A user enters a wrap when they swing the dart around a body part and hold the loop in place with their off (“anchor”) hand. For example, a dragon wrap has the user swing the rope under their dominant (“lead”) armpit and over their anchor shoulder before holding it in place. 

If they were to simply cast the dart from here, the rope would catch on their shoulder and not extend to its full length. Instead, the user must “exit” the wrap by swinging it to re-trace at least some of the wrap’s path. If they then cast the dart while releasing the wrap with their anchor hand, the re-traced portion will straighten out, unwinding the wrap and letting the cast reach its full length. 

A key detail that I hadn’t accounted for, however, was that you don’t need to re-trace the wrap exactly for it to unwind. For example, you can exit a dragon wrap with any action that swings the rope back over your anchor shoulder. This wouldn’t have worked in the old system, because moves like the dragon wrap were represented atomically in the graph. Instead, I needed a way to break down these moves into their component bind points. 


### Hands-On Experimentation

The problems I identified above highlighted a further flaw in my game design process thus far: I was trying to build a system modeled after real life, yet I had no experience with how the real thing behaved. So I did what any reasonable person would do, and built my own model rope dart out of a bunch of old soccer socks. While I could have made a more accurate and functional training dart with other materials, this one was enough to test different motions without risking destroying my room.

### The Final System (Surely)

With a better understanding of how a rope dart should behave and the goal of a maintainable scale, the structure of my redesigned system was actually pretty easy to come up with. Instead of nodes representing unique states, I now have them represent the pre-determined parts of the body that the rope dart binds to. This helps tackle two problems at once: the number of nodes will never increase, and I can properly implement the logic for wraps and how they unravel. 

To handle the problem of different rope and player states at each node, I shifted responsibility to the connections. Now, each node has a single long list of all possible connections to other body nodes, but each connection has a list of conditions that must be met in order to be used. This does slightly increase the number of connections, but there are still fewer than there would be in the old system.

### Demo Feedback

A huge thank you to everyone who played last week’s demo, and especially to those who left feedback! Reviewing the feedback, it seems like a common sentiment was that the control scheme was unintuitive and unwieldy, especially on controller and trackpad. I have greatly simplified the controls across all input devices, and the new layout will be playable in the next version of the demo! 


### Coming Up

Now that I’m entering the back half of my development timeline, I’m hoping to wrap up the rope dart mechanics in the next week or two. My goal for the end of this sprint is to have a full playtest that is more representative of the overall game. That means this next week will also involve concepts and rough drafts for the character, setting, and music, as well as mocking up the rest of the animations for the rope dart functions currently implemented.
